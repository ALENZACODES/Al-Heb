## Section 1 — file `audio/en/topics/reform-judaism-s01.mp3`

[spoken heading] A way of being Jewish that asks questions

Reform Judaism is one of the living branches of Judaism, and the largest Jewish movement in North America. It began about two hundred years ago, when Jews in Germany and then America started asking a hard question out loud: how do we stay fully Jewish and fully part of the world around us, at the same time?

The Reform answer was not to walk away from tradition. It was to study it seriously, keep what gives life meaning, and let go of what no longer does. A practice is kept because it is holy, or beautiful, or because it pulls a person toward a better life — not only because it was always done. That makes Reform Judaism a thinking person's Judaism. Learning is not a step on the way to belonging. Learning is the belonging.

Reform rabbis have written their ideas down several times, in 1885, in 1937, and again in 1999. They were careful to call these writings guides, not creeds. No one is asked to sign them. They mark out the ground the movement stands on: one God, the Torah as the foundation of Jewish life, and the Jewish people, bound to both and still working out what that means.

## Section 2 — file `audio/en/topics/reform-judaism-s02.mp3`

[spoken heading] The door is genuinely open

Every movement says it welcomes people. Reform congregations have spent decades changing their actual rules and habits so that the welcome is real, and you can see it in who is standing in the room.

The partner who is not Jewish is not treated as a guest who wandered in. Interfaith families are part of the congregation, named from the pulpit with respect, and their children are raised inside the community, not at its edge. Since 1983, the movement has recognised the child of one Jewish parent — father or mother — as Jewish when that child is raised as a Jew. It was a break with older law, made on purpose, so that families would not be split by a definition.

People who choose Judaism are received as full Jews, not as permanent newcomers. The word "convert" is used less and less; many prefer "Jew by choice," which is simply accurate. LGBTQ+ Jews marry under the chuppah, lead services, and serve as rabbis and cantors. Jews of colour, still too often asked to explain themselves at the door of Jewish spaces, are working — with the movement's formal backing — to make that question disappear. Women have been ordained as rabbis since 1972 and as cantors since 1975; a woman is called to the Torah, reads from it, and teaches from it on exactly the same terms as a man.

And the person who is not sure? The one who is curious, or returning after thirty years away, or starting from zero as an adult — which describes a good number of the people using this course. A Reform synagogue expects you. Classes for seekers run all year, and nobody is quizzed at the door.

## Section 3 — file `audio/en/topics/reform-judaism-s03.mp3`

[spoken heading] Why spirituality is not a luxury

It is easy to file spirituality under decoration, something for people with quiet lives and spare time. Most lives are neither. People pray hardest in hospital corridors, at kitchen tables after bad news, and in the first weeks of holding a newborn. The need is not decorative. It is closer to hunger.

Spirituality, in a Jewish key, is mostly built from small repeated things. Lighting candles on Friday and feeling the week set down its weight. A blessing said over bread until, one day, it stops feeling like a performance and starts feeling like gratitude with words attached. Music in a room full of people singing the same old melody slightly out of tune, and meaning it. These practices do something argument cannot: they give grief somewhere to go, and joy a shape, and memory a regular appointment. The mourner's Kaddish is said in community, on schedule, because sorrow keeps a schedule of its own and should not have to be carried alone on the days it shows up.

Reform teaching puts it this way: a person meets God in ordinary places — in wonder, in acting justly, in being loved — and answers in ordinary ways. Prayer, study, and treating people decently are all the same conversation. None of it requires believing the right things before you begin. You begin, and the believing, such as it is, catches up or it does not. The practice is not a test you pass. It is a table you sit at.

## Section 4 — file `audio/en/topics/reform-judaism-s04.mp3`

[spoken heading] Hebrew is part of the welcome

There was a time when some Reform congregations prayed mostly in English, and Hebrew nearly left the building. That time is over. The movement's 1999 statement of principles names the study of Hebrew as a way of drawing closer to the sacred texts, and most Reform prayer books now put Hebrew and English side by side, with transliteration so nobody is locked out by the alphabet.

But transliteration is a bridge, not a home. The sound of the Shema in Hebrew, read by your own mouth off the page, is a different experience from reading it in English letters, and most people feel the difference the first time it happens. That is the whole reason this course exists. Teaching an adult to decode Hebrew, letter by letter, at whatever speed their life allows, is itself an act of inclusion. The texts were never meant to belong to a small class of experts. They belong to whoever shows up to learn them.

## Section 5 — file `audio/en/topics/reform-judaism-s05.mp3`

[spoken heading] If you visit a Reform synagogue

You will be greeted, probably by name-hunting volunteers who are genuinely pleased you came. Sit anywhere. The prayer book will have Hebrew on one side and English on the other, and the person leading will usually say page numbers out loud — follow along in whichever language lets you think.

Stand when the congregation stands, if you are able; if you are not able, nobody minds, and accessible seating is a normal thing to ask about, not a special favour. Dress is simpler than most people fear: neat and comfortable is fine. Phones are generally silenced, and on Shabbat many people leave them away entirely. Services include a sermon, which is teaching, and music, which may include instruments — something older tradition avoided on Shabbat and Reform practice welcomed back.

Afterwards there is usually food, and people will talk to you. If you say "I'm learning Hebrew," you will very likely be handed coffee and three opinions about it. That is not a failure of the welcome. That is the welcome, working as intended.
