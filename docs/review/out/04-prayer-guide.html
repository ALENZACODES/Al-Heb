prayer-guide:openingP2 · flagged: file-level note
מַה טֹּבוּ
mah tovu
What happens: The service begins as people arrive, with a few short readings that set the mood. Services start at different points; arriving late is normal.
Your part: Listen and follow along. Nothing here needs to be said aloud with the group.
How congregations differ: Orthodox and many Conservative services include a long preparatory section; many Reform services begin directly with a song and a Barchu.

prayer-guide:pesukei-dezimraP2 · flagged: file-level note
פְּסוּקֵי דְזִמְרָה
pesukei dezimrah
What happens: A block of psalms and praise verses. The best-known piece is Ashrei, which is also said again later in the Shabbat service.
Your part: Read along; Ashrei is often said aloud by the room, line by line.
How congregations differ: Reform services usually shorten or skip this section.

prayer-guide:shema-and-blessingsP2 · flagged: file-level note
שְׁמַע וּבִרְכוֹתֶיהָ
shema uvirkhoteha
What happens: The formal call to prayer (Barchu), then the Shema, the central statement of Jewish faith, with blessings before and after it.
Your part: Answer Barchu when the leader calls. Say the first line of the Shema aloud with the room. After it, "Baruch shem" is said quietly. The V'ahavta follows.
How congregations differ: Some Reform books shorten the blessings around the Shema.

prayer-guide:amidahP2 · flagged: file-level note
עֲמִידָה
amidah
What happens: The main prayer, said standing and silently by each person; on Shabbat morning the leader then repeats part of it aloud (the Kedushah is the congregation's main response).
Your part: Stand, say the Amidah quietly at your own pace. During the leader's repetition, join the Kedushah responses and answer amen.
How congregations differ: Reform and Conservative movement books add the matriarchs to the first blessing. Orthodox services read the full repetition; some Reform services read only parts of it aloud. The Priestly Blessing is performed by kohanim at Musaf on festivals in many Orthodox communities in the diaspora.

prayer-guide:torah-service-openP2 · flagged: file-level note
הוֹצָאַת סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה
hotza'at sefer torah
What happens: The ark is opened, the congregation sings, and the scroll is taken out and carried through the room to the reading desk. This is a good place to follow along: nearly everything is sung.
Your part: Most congregations stand while the ark is open; follow the room. Join the singing; touch the Torah with a prayer book or tallit fringe as it passes if your community does.
How congregations differ: Sephardi and Mizrahi services order the procession differently (the scroll is often carried before these passages). Many Reform services omit Brich Shmei and shorten the rest.

prayer-guide:torah-readingP2 · flagged: file-level note
קְרִיאַת הַתּוֹרָה
keri'at hatorah
What happens: The weekly portion is read from a handwritten scroll, in sections called aliyot. A person is called up for each section and says a blessing before and after it. The scroll has no vowels and no cantillation marks; the reader chants from memory or from a practice text.
Your part: If you are called up (an aliyah): follow the person who guides you, say Barchu, then the blessing. The congregation answers "Baruch Adonai hamevorach" and "Amen". The reading itself is not in this registry (no pointed Torah text is included).
How congregations differ: The blessing words are the same in the three movements. The number of aliyot and who may be called up varies by movement and by congregation.

הַגְבָּהָה וּגְלִילָה
hagbahah ugelilah
What happens: After the reading, the scroll is lifted so the congregation can see the text, then rolled and dressed.
Your part: Stand, look at the text, and sing or say the line.
How congregations differ: Bowing or pointing toward the scroll as it is lifted is a custom in many congregations.

הַפְטָרָה
haftarah
What happens: A passage from the Prophets, linked to the week, is chanted with its own blessings.
Your part: Answer amen to the blessings. The haftarah itself is chanted by one person.
How congregations differ: Reform and Conservative books may shorten the closing blessings.

תְּפִלּוֹת לַקָּהָל
tefilot lakahal
What happens: A short block of prayers for the congregation, for Israel and the country, often followed by the sermon.
Your part: Listen; join amen. Wording varies a lot.
How congregations differ: These prayers are written by each community or movement and are mostly under copyright; they are not in the registry.

prayer-guide:musafP2 · flagged: file-level note
מוּסָף
musaf
What happens: On Shabbat and festivals a second Amidah is said. Orthodox and many Conservative services include it; most Reform services omit it.
Your part: Same as the morning Amidah: say it quietly; join the Kedushah.
How congregations differ: Often omitted in Reform services.

prayer-guide:closingP2 · flagged: file-level note
סִיּוּם
siyum
What happens: The service ends with Aleinu, the Mourner's Kaddish, and a hymn.
Your part: Stand for Aleinu. Mourners say the Kaddish; the room answers amen and "Yehei shmei rabba".
How congregations differ: Order and the choice of closing hymn vary.

prayer-guide:kiddush-lunchP2 · flagged: file-level note
קִדּוּשׁ
kidush
What happens: After services most congregations hold a kiddush: wine or grape juice, then a meal that begins with the blessing over bread.
Your part: Answer amen. Hamotzi is said by one person at the table; everyone then eats.
How congregations differ: Some communities say only V'shamru; some add Zachor.
