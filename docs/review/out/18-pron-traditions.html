A Reform synagogue in the US (suggested setting: sephardi; confidence medium)
Most likely Israeli-based sounds, which the app calls the American-synagogue setting. Expect some Ashkenazi forms in familiar words and songs.
Caveats: In the survey, Reform respondents were the group most likely to choose the Israeli form of the one word asked about, but about a third also use the Ashkenazic form. Individual cantors and older members often keep Ashkenazi habits.
Ask first: Which pronunciation do the cantor and the Torah readers use, and is there a recording I can practise with?

The app's Sephardi-values setting matches the vowels (a, e, i, o, u) but not your community's consonants, which may include pharyngeal het and ayin.

pron-traditions:guide:israel-travelP5 · flagged: draft
Travelling or living in Israel (suggested setting: israeli; confidence high)
Learn the Modern Israeli sounds. Everyday Israeli Hebrew has no pharyngeals for most speakers, a back-of-the-throat r, and mostly final stress.
Caveats: Some older Mizrahi Israelis still use pharyngeal sounds, and Israeli synagogues keep Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi and Yemenite customs side by side. ALEF does not teach Tel Aviv slang or informal fast speech: no source for those was found.
Ask first: Is it for travel talk (Israeli track) or for services in Israel (ask the synagogue)?

שַׁבָּת
shabat
Tav without a dot (ת) (Shabbat / Shabbos)
Israeli: t (Shabbat)

Other communities: Yemenite, Iraqi and some Greek communities: th as in thin; some Spanish-Portuguese and Spanish-Moroccan communities: d, or th as in this

Ayin (ע) (olam / oylom)
Israeli: silent, or a tiny catch like the break in uh-oh (same as alef)

רְאוּבֵן

Resh (ר) (Reuven)
Israeli: a back-of-the-throat r, like French or German; nearly all Israeli Jews use it
American synagogue: the English r (most Americans). Hearing a uvular r in Israel is normal; using the English r is a common marker of a foreign speaker (lane 3's inference).
Ashkenazi: a tapped or trilled r or a uvular r, depending on region
Other communities: Sephardi: a front tapped or trilled r, like Spanish; Immigrants from Arabic-speaking countries (Israel): often keep the trilled r

pron-traditions:sound:stressP5 · flagged: draft
שָׁלוֹם
shalom
Which syllable is stressed (shaLOM / SHOlom)
Israeli: most words on the last syllable; some, such as words of the melech/boker type, on the one before; spoken Israeli drifts toward the second-to-last syllable
American synagogue: last syllable in prayer words (baRUKH, shaLOM), as in Israeli
Ashkenazi: traditionally the second-to-last syllable (SHOlom, BOrukh), but final stress was promoted for Torah reading and public prayer, and many congregations use it
Other communities: Sephardi: keeps the older pattern, which for most words is the last syllable; Yemenite: the Wikipedia page says second-to-last, but tags that claim 'citation needed'; ALEF does not rely on it

Yemenite Hebrew
Pharyngeal het and ayin, as in Arabic.
Gimel without a dot is a voiced throat fricative and with a dot is a j sound.

Mizrahi and Sephardi communities (Middle East, North Africa, Balkans, Anatolia)
Many keep pharyngeal het and sometimes ayin; the details differ by community.
Some read tav as th, and some keep qof distinct (the pages we read name Iraqi, Greek and Georgian examples).
There is no single 'Sephardi accent': the pages stress many variants inside each grouping.
ALEF's 'sephardi' setting is NOT a model of any of these communities. If your synagogue belongs to one, ask a member or the cantor and treat this app as a general guide only.
