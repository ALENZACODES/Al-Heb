Read the Siddur aloud

30 pages of the short texts you meet first. Each page gives a few phrases in large pointed type, taken word for word from the course's text collection, and three boxes that show how the phrase sounds in the Israeli, Sephardi and Ashkenazi ways of reading.

How to practise

  1. Read the Hebrew from the right. Say it aloud, slowly, before you look at a box.
  2. Check yourself against the box for the tradition you are learning; the other boxes show what you may hear in other communities.
  3. Colour a circle each time you read the phrase aloud without stopping. Three circles, then move on.

Where the three traditions part

  • Bet is b with dagesh and v without.
  • Kaf is k with dagesh and kh (like loch) without.
  • Pe is p with dagesh and f without.
  • The dot decides: right dot = sh, left dot = s.
  • Dagesh kal hardens begadkefat letters (only bet, kaf, pe change sound in Israeli Hebrew). Dagesh chazak (doubling) is not pronounced in Modern Hebrew.
  • In General Israeli chet and khaf are one sound [χ]; older Mizrahi speakers keep the pharyngeal chet.
  • In General Israeli ayin is merged with alef. Pharyngeal ayin survives in Sephardi/Mizrahi tradition; Ashkenazi is silent.
  • Nearly all Israeli Jews pronounce resh as a uvular fricative; the English r is the typical American carry-over.
  • Ashkenazi tav without dagesh is s (Shabbos); Sephardi and Israeli tav is always t.
